Question job: choose punctuation from grammatical structure. First find the sentence skeleton. Then choose the mark.

Knowledge Foundation

Core idea: SAT Boundaries questions are sentence-structure questions. Punctuation is the final step. Prove the structure first; then choose only a mark licensed by that structure.

Foundation 1

Build the sentence skeleton

  • A complete clause needs a subject and a finite verb.
  • A finite verb carries tense or agrees with its subject.
  • Some verbs also need an object or complement before the thought is complete.
SubjectThe telescope +Finite verbreveals +Objectnew stars.
Complete

The river rises.

The river is the subject; rises is the finite verb; the thought is complete.

Dependent

Because the river rises

The subject and verb exist, but because makes the clause dependent.

Fix Because the river rises, the fields flood.

Nonfinite trap Researchers studying the samples.

Fix Researchers studying the samples found a pattern.

Why: studying describes researchers; found is the finite verb.

Know each mark's permission and limit

Period / semicolon

  • A semicolon is not a stronger comma.

Foundation 5

Recognize three high-frequency failure families

Comma splice

Wrong The lab closed, the team left.

Fix The lab closed; the team left.

Fragment

Wrong Although the rain stopped.

Fix Although the rain stopped, the game continued.

Double boundary

Wrong The lab closed; and the team left.

Fix The lab closed, and the team left.

StructureDC | IC
Correct pattern

DC , IC

StructureIC | essential DC
Correct pattern

IC DC
Closest trap

Why: In this sentence, the because-clause supplies the essential reason for the closing.

Subtype 1

Independent-Clause Join

IC Join Table

Question job: Join two complete sentences legally.

Recognition cue: Both sides have a subject, finite verb, and complete thought.

Decision steps
  1. Prove the left side is an independent clause.
  2. Prove the right side is an independent clause.
  3. Choose a period or semicolon, or use comma + FANBOYS.
  4. Check transition punctuation separately.

Why this works: Treating both sides as complete clauses reduces the choice to a small legal join table.

Boundary: A comma alone cannot join two independent clauses. A coordinating conjunction without its comma also fails.
Closest trap: Comma splice or conjunction-only run-on.
Self-check: Can both sides stand alone exactly as written?

Portable takeaway: If both sides can stand alone, a comma alone is impossible.

Subtype 2

Dependent-Clause Attachment

Dependency Attachment

Question job: Attach a dependent clause without creating a fragment or run-on.

Recognition cue: A subordinator such as although, because, when, while, if, or since makes a clause dependent.

Decision steps
  1. Circle the subordinator.
  2. Find where the dependent clause ends.
  3. Identify DC + IC or IC + DC order.
  4. Apply the position rule and reread the full sentence.

Why this works: A subordinator changes clause status even when the clause contains a subject and verb.

Boundary: A dependent clause may contain a subject and verb but still cannot stand alone. An initial dependent clause normally takes a comma before the independent clause.
Closest trap: Treating the dependent clause as a complete sentence.
Self-check: Which clause depends on the other, and where does it end?

Portable takeaway: Circle the subordinator before judging punctuation.
